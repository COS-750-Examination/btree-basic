## The key idea

A B-tree stays short for two reasons:

1. **It only grows at the root.** A split at the root adds a level above everything, so all leaves stay on the same level. No rotations or rebalancing are needed.
2. **Every node branches widely.** Each node except the root has at least ⌈m/2⌉ children, so the number of keys a tree can hold multiplies by at least ⌈m/2⌉ per level.

So the height is about **log_m(n)** when nodes are full and at most about **log\_⌈m/2⌉(n)** when they are half full.

## Worked example

**m = 100, one million keys.**

- **Nodes full** (99 keys each): 3 levels hold at most 100 × 100 × 100 − 1 = 999,999 keys, just one short, so the tree needs **4 levels**.
- **Nodes half full** (50 children each): a fifth level would need more than 12 million keys, so the tree still has **at most 4 levels**.
- **A perfectly balanced binary search tree:** 2 children per node, so about **20 levels**.

Each level is one disk read. Four reads instead of twenty is why databases and file systems use B-trees.

## Watch out

- **Bigger nodes don't make searching slower.** Comparing keys inside a node happens in memory and is quick. The slow part is reading each node from disk, and a short tree reads fewer nodes.
- **B-trees don't rotate.** That's AVL and red-black trees. A B-tree stays balanced through splits and merges alone.

## Check yourself

A 2-3 tree (order 3) holds **1,000 keys**. Roughly how tall can it be, at best and at worst?

## Answer

**At best 7 levels, at worst 9.** With full nodes (2 keys, 3 children), h levels hold at most 3ʰ − 1 keys: 3⁶ − 1 = 728 is too few and 3⁷ − 1 = 2,186 is enough, so at least **7 levels**. With nodes holding just 1 key, h levels need at least 2ʰ − 1 keys: 2¹⁰ − 1 = 1,023 is more than 1,000, so at most **9 levels**. Either way it is short, and every leaf is on the same level.
