## The key idea

Moving the middle key up can make the **parent** overflow too. Split it the same way, and keep going up. If the **root** splits, its middle key becomes a **new root**, and the tree grows **one level taller, at the top**.

When an internal node splits, its **children are shared out** too: the left half keeps the children to the left of the middle key, and the right half takes the rest.

## Worked example

Order 3 (at most 2 keys). Root `[20 | 40]` with leaves `[5 | 12]`, `[25 | 30]` and `[50 | 60]`. **Insert 55.**

1. `[50 | 55 | 60]` overflows. **55** moves up. The leaves are now `[50]` and `[60]`.
2. The root becomes `[20 | 40 | 55]`: **3 keys, overflow**.
3. The root splits around **40**. `[20]` and `[55]` become its children, and **40** is the **new root**.
4. Share out the four leaves: `[20]` keeps `[5 | 12]` and `[25 | 30]`; `[55]` takes `[50]` and `[60]`.

The tree went from 2 levels to **3**, and every leaf is still on the same level.

## Watch out

- **Only the root split adds a level.** Splits lower down make the tree wider, not taller.
- **Count children after each split:** a node with k keys needs exactly k + 1 children.

## Check yourself

Start again from root `[20 | 40]` with leaves `[5 | 12]`, `[25 | 30]` and `[50 | 60]`. **Insert 8.** What is the new root, and what are its children?

## Answer

`[5 | 8 | 12]` splits and **8** moves up, so the root becomes `[8 | 20 | 40]` and splits around **20**. New root `[20]`, with children `[8]` (over `[5]` and `[12]`) and `[40]` (over `[25 | 30]` and `[50 | 60]`).
